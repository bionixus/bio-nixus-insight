Mystery shopping is the most direct way to check whether the service customers receive in your Saudi stores, branches, showrooms and call centres matches the standard you trained for. It is also easy to get wrong: too few visits, scenarios staff can spot, and recordings that create legal exposure. This guide is for retail, banking, hospitality and customer-experience teams comparing mystery shopping companies in Saudi Arabia. It explains what a sound programme measures, how the Personal Data Protection Law shapes the design, how many visits you need and what to ask a provider before you sign.

The guide is part of the BioNixus industry insights hub for B2B and B2C research, alongside our work on retail market research in Saudi Arabia. Each law, standard and statistic below links to its official or peer-reviewed source, and every source is listed again under References.

Key takeaways

  • Mystery shopping shows whether your standards were delivered at the counter, on the phone or online. It is not a measure of how customers feel: across three service-retail chains, shopper scores were not substantially related to customers’ own evaluations or to sales (Blessing & Natter, 2019).
  • Saudi institutions already rely on the method. The Saudi Central Bank requires financial institutions to monitor front-line staff through periodic or confidential visits (SAMA, Rule 15), and Adaa uses “mystery beneficiary” visits to check government services (Adaa).
  • Under the PDPL, photos and videos of an individual are personal data, and collection methods must not involve deception (PDPL, Articles 1 and 11). Score outlets and processes, not named employees; tell your own staff in advance; leave covert audio and video out.
  • One or two visits per outlet is noise. Peer-reviewed studies found that 3–4 shops per outlet are much less reliable than commercial studies assume, and that store scores swing between waves (Finn & Kayandé, 1999; Dawes & Sharp, 2000).
  • Sample where your customers are: the Riyadh, Makkah and Eastern Province regions hold 68% of Saudi Arabia’s population (GASTAT census 2022, via SPA).
  • Before you hire, get the scenario logic, shopper profiles, quality-control evidence and data-handling plan in writing.

What do mystery shopping companies Saudi Arabia’s brands hire actually deliver?

ESOMAR, the global association for research and insight, describes mystery shoppers as people “trained and/or briefed to observe, experience and measure any customer service process by acting as a prospective customer and undertaking a series of pre-determined tasks to assess performance against specific criteria” (ESOMAR, Mystery Shopping Studies). The UK Market Research Society (MRS) adds that the evaluator does not declare their presence, and that results are better used “for directing training and for rewards schemes rather than for disciplinary purposes” (MRS Guideline: Conducting Mystery Shopping, 2024).

A well-run programme gives you four things:

  • Standards compliance: whether the store, branch, showroom or call centre did what your service blueprint says, from greeting and needs questions to explanation, offer and close.
  • Process and environment checks: waiting time, cleanliness, signage, price display and whether a proper receipt was issued.
  • Channel consistency: the same scenario tested in store, by phone, on the website and in the app, so you can see where the journey breaks.
  • Competitive benchmarks: anonymous visits to competitors, reported in aggregate only.

What it cannot do is stand in for your customers. Finn and Kayandé note that mystery shopping is “best suited when assessing objective characteristics of outlet operations”, such as the store environment, while customer surveys suit more subjective judgements of service quality (Journal of Retailing, 1999). Blessing and Natter went further: in large-scale data from three service-retail chains they found no substantial correlation between shopper assessments and customer evaluations, and shopper scores did not predict sales, while customer satisfaction did (Journal of Retailing, 2019). Treat mystery shopping as a standards and coaching tool, and run customer feedback alongside it when the question is loyalty or revenue.

Who already uses mystery shopping in Saudi Arabia?

Three examples show how the method fits the Kingdom’s institutions and rules:

  • Banking and finance. The Saudi Central Bank’s Financial Consumer Protection Principles and Rules require financial institutions to “monitor the performance of front-line employees through (periodic/confidential) visits to the branches, call center, and collection staff (including third party employees)”, with semi-annual reports to senior management (SAMA Rulebook, Rule 15). The same rules require the list of fees and commissions and the complaint-handling mechanism to be displayed in branches (Rules 9 and 18), and both are easy for a shopper to verify. See our page on financial services market research in Saudi Arabia.
  • Government services. The National Center for Performance Measurement (Adaa) lists the “mystery beneficiary” among its beneficiary-experience tools, used for “assessing the entities compliance with service standards, and checking gaps in their service delivery procedures” (Adaa, Beneficiary Experience). In Ramadan 1445H, Adaa combined surveys with secret visits to measure more than 46 Umrah and Prophet’s Mosque visit services across more than 16 government entities (Adaa). Related methods are covered on our public sector market research in Saudi Arabia page.
  • Retail compliance checks. Since 4 December 2021, VAT taxpayers resident in the Kingdom have had to issue electronic invoices, and simplified (B2C) tax invoices must carry a QR code (ZATCA). Whether the receipt is a compliant e-invoice is a factual check a shopper can record at the till.

The same approach applies to showrooms, hotels, restaurants and telecom stores. Digital channels belong in scope too: the Communications, Space and Technology Commission reported internet penetration of 99% in 2024 (CST, Saudi Internet Report 2024).

What do Saudi data-protection and cybercrime laws mean for mystery shopping?

BioNixus is a research company, not a law firm. This section summarises official texts so you can brief your legal team. It is not legal advice.

The Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) was issued by Royal Decree M/19 of 9/2/1443H and amended by Royal Decree M/148 of 5/9/1444H (SDAIA). It came into force on 14 September 2023, and the one-year grace period for compliance ended on 14 September 2024 (SDAIA guide to the PDPL). It covers processing in the Kingdom and processing of data about people residing in the Kingdom from outside it (Article 2). These provisions matter most for a mystery shopping programme:

RuleWhat the text saysWhat it means for your programme
PDPL Article 1(4)Personal data includes “photos and videos of an individual”.Any recording of staff is personal data, so default to no covert audio or video.
PDPL Article 11(2)–(4)Collection methods must not “involve any deception, misleading or extortion”, content must be limited “to the minimum amount necessary”, and data no longer needed must be destroyed.Score the outlet and the process, not a named employee. Keep staff names out unless your own staff have been told and counsel has signed off the legal basis.
PDPL Article 27; Implementing Regulation Article 30Research use without consent is possible where data does not specifically identify the person, or where evidence of identity is destroyed before disclosure; research data should be minimal and pseudonymised where possible.Remove identifiers before reports leave the field team.
Implementing Regulation Article 16Relying on legitimate interest requires a documented assessment before processing, and the processing must be within the data subject’s reasonable expectations.Ask your provider for the assessment, not just an assurance.
Implementing Regulation Articles 17 and 24A processor agreement must include the processor’s duty to notify the controller of a breach without undue delay, and the controller must notify SDAIA within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach that may cause harm.Write a concrete breach-notice deadline for the provider into the contract.
PDPL Article 29; Transfer RegulationTransfers outside the Kingdom need an adequate level of protection and must be limited to the minimum personal data needed.Find out where shopper reports, receipt photos and any audio are stored and processed.

The details are in the PDPL Implementing Regulation and the Regulation on Personal Data Transfer outside the Kingdom (version 2.0, August 2024). Penalties are material: violations of the law or its regulations can draw a warning or a fine of up to SAR 5 million, which can be doubled for repeat violations (PDPL Article 36).

Recording carries a separate criminal risk. Article 3 of the Anti-Cyber Crime Law (Royal Decree M/17 of 8/3/1428H) makes “invasion of privacy through the misuse of camera-equipped mobile phones and the like” punishable by up to one year in prison and a fine of up to SAR 500,000, or either (Bureau of Experts at the Council of Ministers). Briefing shoppers to film staff on a phone puts a programme close to that clause, and a structured form, timestamps and the receipt usually give you the evidence you need without it.

Professional codes point the same way. When a client shops its own organisation, ESOMAR requires staff to be told in advance that shopping will take place within a future period, what its objectives are, whether individuals will be identified and whether any recording will be made, and findings must not be used to discipline or otherwise disadvantage the individuals involved (ESOMAR). The MRS Code of Conduct allows mystery shopping to be recorded only in own-organisation programmes where staff have been informed (MRS Code of Conduct 2023, rule 29). For competitors, whose staff cannot be told, MRS guidance says personal information about employees cannot be collected, visits should be kept as short as possible, and data must be reported at an aggregated level (MRS, 2024). The Mystery Shopping Providers Association’s guidelines add that disciplinary action should not be taken solely on mystery shopping results (MSPA), and research with managers and agencies found that employees’ acceptance of mystery shopping is critical if its results are to be taken seriously (Wilson, 2001).

Shoppers need protecting as well. MRS asks agencies to keep scenarios safe, to warn shoppers that caller ID, personal payment cards and website cookies can reveal who they are, and to reimburse purchases promptly (MRS, 2024).

How do you run a mystery shopping programme in Saudi Arabia, step by step?

Use these eight steps as the minimum you hold any provider to. Each one maps to the evidence in this guide.

  1. Define the decision. Write down what the results will change, such as coaching, a franchise or partner audit, a regulatory self-check or a competitor benchmark, and the level you need results at: network, region, format or individual outlet.
  2. Turn standards into observable items. Convert your service blueprint into yes/no and timed items. MRS advises that most questions should record what happened at the point of contact rather than how the shopper felt.
  3. Write credible scenarios. Give each scenario one realistic customer need that should trigger the behaviours you trained, and match shoppers to the customer profile, language and product familiarity the scenario needs.
  4. Settle the data plan before fieldwork. Tell your own staff that mystery shopping will happen, decide what identifies an outlet rather than a person, leave covert audio and video out by default, and agree where data is stored and for how long.
  5. Size and spread the visits. Set visits per outlet from the reporting level, and spread them across cities, days, times and channels, because the time of day, week or season can change the service staff deliver.
  6. Pilot and calibrate. Run a small pilot, check that scenarios are credible and forms unambiguous, and align shoppers on what counts as a pass before the main wave.
  7. Field with quality control. Have shoppers complete forms as soon as they leave, keep receipts and timestamps as evidence, and back-check a share of visits.
  8. Report for action. Report at the level your sample supports, show the number of visits behind every score, and route findings to coaching and process owners rather than discipline.

How many visits do you need before the scores mean something?

There is no universal number, but the evidence is clear that single visits mislead:

  • Reliability is lower than buyers assume. Using generalizability theory, Finn and Kayandé found that mystery shoppers give reasonably reliable ratings of retail outlets, “although much less reliable than seems to be assumed for commercial studies using 3–4 shoppers” (Journal of Retailing, 1999).
  • Store scores move between waves. Dawes and Sharp analysed four quarters of data from a programme covering more than 200 outlets and found “considerable variation in store scores between waves”, concluding that mystery shopping surveys “should seldom be used to assess changes in service performance at individual store level” (Australasian Journal of Market Research, 2000).
  • Memory distorts the record. Morrison, Colman and Preston showed that the way assessors encode, store and retrieve what they saw, and the differences between assessors, threaten accuracy, and they set out ways to reduce memory errors (Journal of the Market Research Society, 1997).

Practical rules follow from that evidence:

  • Decide the reporting level first. Results by network, region or format need far fewer visits per outlet than an outlet league table.
  • Favour factual items, such as whether the receipt was issued, whether the price was displayed and how long the wait was, over impressions.
  • Rotate shoppers across outlets so one person’s style does not become an outlet’s score.
  • Check reliability after the first wave, for example by comparing shoppers who visited the same outlet, before you attach targets or bonuses to the scores.

Geography matters as much as volume. Saudi Arabia’s 2022 census counted 32,175,224 people, 58.4% of them Saudi nationals and 41.6% non-Saudis. The Riyadh, Makkah and Eastern Province regions hold 68% of the population; Riyadh is the largest city, followed by Jeddah, Makkah, Madinah and Dammam; and 63% of Saudi nationals are under 30 (GASTAT census 2022, via SPA). A programme that samples one city cannot speak for a national network, and shopper panels should reflect who your customers are: Saudi nationals and residents of other nationalities, women and men, Arabic and English speakers.

How do you keep results comparable across Saudi Arabia, Dubai and the GCC?

Regional brands often want one scorecard for Riyadh, Jeddah, Dubai and other Gulf cities. That works if you harmonise the instrument and localise the legal checks:

  • Same scenarios, same pass rules. Translate and back-translate scenarios and items, and keep definitions, such as what counts as a greeting and when the wait-time clock starts, identical in every country.
  • Different data-protection regimes. In the UAE, Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the protection of personal data took effect on 2 January 2022 (UAE Legislation) and excludes free-zone companies that have their own data-protection legislation (Article 2). The Dubai International Financial Centre has its own Data Protection Law (DIFC Law No. 5 of 2020), and Abu Dhabi Global Market has its own Data Protection Regulations 2021 (ADGM Office of Data Protection), so outlets in different UAE jurisdictions can fall under different rules.
  • Recording is a criminal-law question in the UAE too. Article 44 of Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021 punishes using information technology to invade someone’s privacy without consent, including recording conversations or taking photos of others in any public or private place, with at least six months’ imprisonment and/or a fine of AED 150,000 to AED 500,000 (UAE Legislation).
  • Report markets side by side. Outlet counts and visit numbers differ by country, so a pooled GCC average can hide a weak market.

What drives the cost of a mystery shopping programme?

Quotes vary because programmes vary. These are the drivers to compare line by line:

  • Outlets, visits and waves: the core volume, set by the reporting level you chose.
  • Geography: the number of cities and how far apart the outlets are.
  • Scenario length and complexity: a short price check takes less fieldwork than a test drive, an account-opening enquiry or a hotel stay.
  • Purchases: whether shoppers buy, return or refund, and who keeps the goods.
  • Channels and languages: in store, phone, web and app; Arabic, English or both.
  • Shopper profile: the harder a customer profile is to match, the more recruitment it takes.
  • Evidence and data handling: receipts, timestamps, storage location and retention requirements.
  • Reporting: a dashboard, outlet-level reports, coaching packs or a board summary.

Ask each provider to price these drivers separately, so you can trade visits against outlets or channels without losing the reliability you need.
