On 13 November 2026, [Healthcare IT Today published a guest article](https://www.healthcareittoday.com/2026/11/13/when-your-billing-team-is-overseas-what-data-residency-laws-teach-about-ehr-access/) by Mohammad Alsaadany, Director at BioNixus, on what data-residency laws teach when a billing team in another country can open a patient's electronic health record. The same questions now sit on pharmaceutical and healthcare research briefs across the Gulf and the wider Middle East and North Africa.

Being able to see a file, being required to keep that file in a named country, and signing a contract about how the file is handled are three different problems.

> "A signed BAA is not proof of residency," Alsaadany wrote, referring to the U.S. business associate agreement that many health-data contracts still treat as the end of the discussion.

The piece also warned against treating "PDPL" as if it were a single residency statute. Personal-data laws in the region set conditions for transfers. Some health-sector laws go further and limit where certain records may be stored or sent at all.

Those distinctions matter once a study leaves the sponsor's office. An interview recorded in Riyadh may be transcribed abroad. A dashboard or a quality-control login in another country may expose identifiable comments, audio, or sample identifiers. None of those steps is the same as storing the file in-country, and none is permitted just because a services agreement is on file. This is not legal advice. Official texts bind, and counsel in the relevant market should review the data map before a fieldwork brief is locked.

## Seeing a file is not the same as moving it

The Healthcare IT Today article asked hospital and billing leaders to draw the data path: who can open the record, from where, and whether a copy leaves the country when they do. Research operations need the same diagram. Access control answers who may open a recording, transcript, or shipment file. Residency answers where the data is stored and whether a copy may be sent abroad. A contractual promise is not a location. If a project manager abroad can download an interview file, that is already a transfer question in many of the laws below, even if the source copy still sits on a server in the Gulf.

## Saudi Arabia: transfer conditions, not a blank cheque

Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law is published in English by the Saudi Data and AI Authority (SDAIA). [Article 23 of the official English text](https://sdaia.gov.sa/en/SDAIA/about/Documents/PersonalDataProtectionLaw.pdf) limits access to health data to the extent necessary and in accordance with the regulations. [Article 29](https://sdaia.gov.sa/en/SDAIA/about/Documents/PersonalDataProtectionLaw.pdf) sets conditions for disclosing or transferring personal data outside the Kingdom, including a link to an agreement, interest, or obligation; no prejudice to national security or vital interests; appropriate protection at least equivalent to the law, as assessed by the competent authority; and sending only the necessary data, with a narrow exception for extreme necessity to protect the data subject's vital interests.

SDAIA has also published an [Implementing Regulation](https://sdaia.gov.sa/en/SDAIA/about/Documents/ImplementingRegulation.pdf), including Article 26 on health data, and a [separate regulation on transfers](https://sdaia.gov.sa/Documents/RegulationonPersonalDataEN.pdf) covering scientific research, the adequacy list, and risk assessment for continuous or widespread transfers of sensitive data, which includes health data. The law entered into force on 14 September 2023, and a one-year grace period ended on 14 September 2024. For a study in the Kingdom, the brief should name where audio, transcripts, and identifiers will be stored, who can open them, and which transfer pathway is being used. A PDPL clause in a contract is not a completed transfer assessment.

## The UAE: a general data law and a stricter health-ICT law

The United Arab Emirates has two federal layers that research teams often collapse into one.

[Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021](https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/1972) is the first federal personal-data statute. The [official UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/digital-uae/data/data-protection-laws), updated on 4 December 2025, states that the law applies to processing inside or outside the country, that it sets requirements for the cross-border transfer and sharing of personal data, and that it came into force on 2 January 2022. The UAE Data Office, established under Federal Decree-Law No. 44 of 2021, is the federal regulator. Individual transfer-article numbers are not quoted here, because they were not re-checked line by line from the official PDF for this article; the binding text is the official legislation.

[Federal Law No. 2 of 2019](https://uaelegislation.gov.ae/en/legislations/1209), on the use of information and communication technology in health fields, is the stricter layer. The [official government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/digital-uae/whole-of-government-approach/digital-health/ict-in-the-health-sector) and the official legislation confirm that the law applies in the UAE, including its free zones. Article 13 provides that health data and information related to health services provided in the State may not be stored, processed, generated, or transferred outside the State except by a decision of the Health Authority, in coordination with the Ministry. Article 12 points to ministerial controls for in-country storage. Article 20's 25-year retention period, measured from the last health procedure, is a hospital-records rule, not a market-research rule.

Whether Law No. 2 of 2019 applies to a given interview or chart-review dataset depends on whether the data is health data related to health services provided in the State. A recording that entered a hospital system is a different object from a de-identified professional interview, and UAE counsel should check the actual data. A vendor in a free zone, including the Dubai International Financial Centre, does not automatically sit outside the 2019 law, even though DIFC has its own Data Protection Law No. 5 of 2020. Cabinet Resolution No. 32 of 2020 is the executive regulation of the health-ICT law and should be read before any claim about hosting or transfer permissions.

## Qatar: a more open transfer default, with a tighter rule for health data

Qatar's Law No. 13 of 2016 on Personal Data Privacy Protection was published in Official Gazette No. 15 of 2016. The official Arabic text is available through [Al Meezan](https://www.almeezan.qa/LawArticles.aspx?LawID=7121). The National Cyber Security Agency now hosts the [Personal Data Privacy Protection Office](https://assurance.ncsa.gov.qa/en/library/privacy).

English explanations of Article 15 describe a default that is more permissive than the Saudi or UAE transfer rules: controllers are generally not to restrict cross-border flows unless the processing would breach the law or may cause serious damage to the data or to privacy. English explanations of Article 16 treat health data as data of a special nature and generally require permission from the competent department before it is processed. This article did not re-extract the official Arabic wording of those articles line by line, so operational decisions should be taken from the Gazette text or a qualified translation. If a Doha study file contains health data, do not assume that a general "Qatar allows transfers" shorthand is enough.

## Bahrain: transfers are prohibited unless a listed pathway applies

Bahrain's [Law No. 30 of 2018](https://www.pdp.gov.bh/en/regulations.html), published in official English by the Personal Data Protection Authority, defines sensitive personal data to include information in relation to an individual's health. Article 5 generally prohibits processing that data without the data subject's consent, with listed exceptions, including certain medical and healthcare-management purposes carried out by a licensed medical professional or another person bound by a legal duty of confidentiality.

Article 12 prohibits transferring personal data outside the Kingdom except where the destination is on the Authority's published adequacy list, or the Authority authorises the transfer case by case after assessing the data, the purpose and duration of processing, the countries involved, and the protections available there. Article 13 adds exemptions, including consent, certain public registers, transfers necessary for a contract with or in the interest of the data subject, vital interests, and legal obligations or claims. The Authority may also authorise a transfer, subject to safeguards such as a contract, to a country without adequate protection. The official English text records that the law was promulgated on 12 July 2018 and took effect one year after the first day of the month following Official Gazette publication. If a transcript or recording will leave Bahrain, the brief should state the Article 5 basis and the Article 12 or 13 pathway being used.

## Egypt: the grace period has now run out

Egypt's Law No. 151 of 2020 is the country's personal-data protection statute. Executive regulations were issued as Decree No. 816 of 2025 and published in the Official Gazette on 1 November 2025 (issue 244, annex "A"), entering into force on 2 November 2025. Legal briefings from A&O Shearman on 18 November 2026 and from Baker McKenzie in October 2026 report a one-year grace period that ended around the start of November 2026. The briefings disagree on the exact end date (around 1 November 2026 versus 31 October 2026) and on which office formally issued the decree. This article did not retrieve the full official English text of the regulations, so cross-border and sensitive-data licensing conditions should be read from the official Arabic text or from Egyptian counsel.

Egypt now has a general data-protection law in force, with executive regulations that have completed their published start and, according to those briefings, a grace period that has ended. A study that sends personal data from Egypt to a vendor abroad should no longer be planned as if the law were still pending.

## What this means for a research or operations brief

The Healthcare IT Today article asked readers to stop treating "PDPL" as one residency rule. Saudi Arabia conditions outbound transfers and treats health data as sensitive. The UAE can block storage or transfer of certain health data without a Health Authority decision. Qatar's general transfer posture is more open, but health data is treated as special. Bahrain starts from a prohibition on outbound transfers unless a listed pathway applies. Egypt's framework is no longer in a grace period.

Sponsors briefing outsourced interviews, chart work, sample logistics, or a regional operations hub should put three lines in the document: where each class of file will be stored, who can open it and from which country, and whether a copy is created when they do.

BioNixus was founded in 2012 and, as of 2026, works with 118 clients in 48 countries and is running 127 studies this year. These questions come up most often after a protocol and vendor list are already chosen. That is late. The cheaper moment to draw the data path is before the first recruiter is briefed.

## What to watch

Watch SDAIA's adequacy list and transfer guidance, including risk assessments for ongoing transfers of sensitive data. Watch the UAE Data Office for implementing instructions under the 2021 law, and Health Authority decisions under Article 13 of the 2019 health-ICT law. Watch Egypt's personal-data regulator for the first enforcement cycle now that firm briefings say the grace period has ended, and treat the official Gazette text as the source if a briefing and the Gazette diverge. Qatar's privacy office and Bahrain's Personal Data Protection Authority may also update adequacy lists or authorisations. Kuwait and Oman are not surveyed here; check their rules separately when a study includes them.

## What to do next

Map viewing versus moving for every vendor on the study: field apps, transcription, translation, coding, quality control, sample files, and dashboards.

Name approved geographies in the brief and in the contract. If a file must stay in-country, say so in those words.

Check health-sector rules separately from the general personal-data law, especially in the UAE.

Ask where logs originate, which subcontractors can open the file, and whether a download is possible.

Take advice from counsel in the country where the data is collected. This is a map of public rules, not an opinion letter.
